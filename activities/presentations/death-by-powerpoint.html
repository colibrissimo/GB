Check the claim: "90% of what you said was gone within 30 seconds"

Phillips gives no source for this number. What is well established is how short working memory is: in a classic experiment, people who were kept busy for 18 seconds forgot about 90% of three-letter strings (Peterson & Peterson, 1959). That was letters in a laboratory, not slides in a talk. The idea is solid; the exact number is his.

Try it: use PowerPoint the way it was meant to be used

Ice cream is a frozen foam. During freezing, the mix is whipped so that air bubbles are trapped between the ice crystals and the fat droplets. The amount of air added is called the overrun. In cheap ice cream the overrun can reach 100%, which means that up to half of the volume you buy is air. Premium ice cream contains less air, so it feels denser and melts more slowly.

Check the claim: "what will be remembered is zero"

He says zero, then "very close to zero". The research he names (John Sweller, Richard Mayer) found that people who heard an explanation and read the same words on screen learned less than people who only heard it. Less, not nothing. Short labels next to a picture did not cause the problem. So a one-line claim is fine; a paragraph you read out is not.

Wait. Last time, Michael Alley said every title should be a full sentence. Phillips says no sentences, and small titles. Who is right?

Mostly, they agree. Phillips's rule is the biggest thing should be the most important thing. In a topic slide, the title ("Ice cream") is the biggest thing and the least important, so he shrinks it. In an Alley slide, the title is the message ("Cheap ice cream can be up to half air"), so it has earned its size.

And the sentences Phillips attacks are paragraphs you read out while you talk. One short claim on screen, explained in your own words, is not that.

Use both: one claim per slide as the title, evidence underneath, nothing else.

Check the claim: "PowerPoint is not supposed to have white backgrounds"

This is advice from experience, not from a study, and it depends on the room. On Zoom, or in a dim room, dark slides with light text work very well. In a bright room with a weak projector, dark slides can turn grey and hard to read. Alley's own templates are light. The rule underneath is the one that matters: the screen should not outshine you, because you are the presentation.

Check the claim: "500% more energy" and "the magic number is six"

The 500% comes from his own audience: 1.2 seconds against 0.2 seconds. That is time, not energy; nobody measured energy. The effect itself is real and has a name, subitizing (Kaufman and colleagues, 1949): a small group of things is seen at once, a bigger one has to be counted. Most studies put the limit at about four, not six (Trick & Pylyshyn, 1994). Your own chart above is the best check you have. Either way, six is a ceiling, not a target.

One file cannot do two jobs

Under the video, a viewer made a fair point. We ask our slides to do two different things:

Support a talk. The audience listens to you, and the slide shows the one thing they must see.
Be a report. Someone reads it alone, later, and it must make sense without you.

A slide that tries to do both does neither. Your teacher's rule: the main points go on the screen; the details go in a handout (or in the notes under each slide).
