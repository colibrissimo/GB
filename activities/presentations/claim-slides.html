Researchers looked at several thousand engineering and science slides. Almost two thirds looked like this one.

What should the audience remember from this slide?

Nobody can say, and that is the problem. The slide never decides. Every bullet is a topic, not an idea: "Results" does not tell you what the results were.

Garner, Alley, Wolfe & Zappe, ASEE Annual Conference, 2011

An assertion-evidence slide makes one claim and shows the proof

1An emulsifier stops the oil and water from separating
2a picture, a diagram or a graph
1 · The claim. One full sentence, with a verb. Two lines maximum.
2 · The evidence. A picture, a diagram or a graph that proves the claim. No bullet list.
3 · You. Explain the evidence out loud. Your script stays in your head (or your notes), not on the slide.

Claim slides helped both the audience and the speakers

The audience

0

engineering students heard the same recorded lecture on MRI. Half saw topic + list slides, half saw claim + evidence slides.

The claim group understood more, had fewer misconceptions, and remembered more one week later.

Garner, Alley et al., International Journal of Engineering Education, 2013

The speakers

You

Students who built claim + evidence slides understood their own topic better than students who built topic + list slides.

Why? You cannot write the claim until you know what you are claiming.

Aippersbach, Alley & Garner, ASEE Annual Conference, 2013

A fair question: who did this research?

Mostly Alley's own group. And the audience study used one recorded lecture, not live talks. That does not make it wrong, but a good scientist notices it. Ask the same question about every number you put on a slide.
