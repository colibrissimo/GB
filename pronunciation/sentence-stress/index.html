Sentence stress

Part 2 of 2: from the word to the sentence. Part 1 put one strong syllable in each word. Now some whole words go strong, and the rest get squeezed.

The rule

In a sentence, English stresses the words that carry the meaning and squeezes the words that only hold the grammar.

Stressed
content words		nouns · main verbs · adjectives · adverbs · question words · prepositions of two syllables or more (before, under)
Squeezed
function words		the, a, an · I, it, we, they · is, are, have, can, should · and, but, so · short prepositions (of, in, for, to)
The telegram test
Keep only the stressed words. You can still understand the sentence.

The brewer roasts the rye and the barley.  🔊 Listen

brewer · roasts · rye · barley  → still a message.
the · the · and · the  → nothing at all.

Part 1 still applies. Inside a stressed word, only its stressed syllable takes the beat: the BREWer, not the brewER; the BARley, not the barLEY. Sentence stress sits on top of word stress. It never replaces it.

1 · Keep the beat

English keeps the time between two stressed syllables roughly the same, however many weak syllables are squeezed in between. French gives every syllable its own time, so a longer sentence takes longer. In English it takes a bit longer, but much less than you think.

The brewer / roasts the / rye and the / barley.

•●•  ●•  ●••  ●•

Four beats. One weak syllable in one gap, two in another, and every gap takes the same time. Tap the table on each capital and say the sentence to the tapping.

Brew beer. 🔊 Listen
Brew the beer. 🔊 Listen
We brew the beer. 🔊 Listen
We can brew the beer. 🔊 Listen
We should have brewed the beer. 🔊 Listen
We should have been brewing the beer. 🔊 Listen
We should have been brewing the beer in the cellar. 🔊 Listen

Tap a steady beat, two taps a line (three on the last one). The sixth line has four times as many syllables as the first and takes nowhere near four times as long: we should have been and -ing the are squeezed into the gaps.

The last one is Jane Austen's opening line from Pride and Prejudice (1813), with a single man swapped for a brewer. Twelve function words and nine content words, and the nine are all you need.
