The sound Schwa

The most common vowel in English has no name of its own, no letter of its own, and no sound you would call a sound.

The rule

ə

schwa, from Hebrew sva: the void, the nothing.

The most frequent vowel sound in the English language is named after emptiness, because that is what it is: the sound a vowel collapses into when nobody is leaning on it.

Two facts and you have the whole thing:

① The schwa appears only in syllables that are not stressed. Never in a stressed one. So it is not really a vowel you learn, it is what is left when the stress goes elsewhere.

② In French it is roughly the e of demain.

Where the stress is NOT, the vowel goes to nothing.

3 · One rule does most of the work

In British English, -er, -or and -our endings are /ə/
No r sound. No stress. Nothing.

Learn it once and you have already done half the vocabulary of the semester:

beer brewer water barley lager cellar porter cooper filtered beverage

consumer flavour factory grocery container preservative colour sugar butter water

flavour and colour: the British -our is the same nothing as -er. The u is not doing anything.

4 · But that is not what I hear

Read this before you decide the rule is wrong.

You have heard water, butter and consumer a thousand times in series and YouTube videos, with a clear, strong r at the end. So this page looks wrong to you.

It is not wrong. You have been listening to Americans.

British English uses /ə/ in words ending -er, -or and -our, while in American English the r is pronounced. American is a rhotic accent: every written r gets said, with the tongue curled back.

Nobody is making a mistake. There are two accents, and your ears have been trained on one of them by everything you watch. That is not a problem, it is an advantage, as long as you know which one you are doing.

What actually sounds odd is switching between the two inside one sentence.
